Why are nonviolent campaigns winning less often?

For most of the last century, mass campaigns that set out to topple a government, end an occupation or win independence became steadily more likely to succeed, as long as they stayed nonviolent. In the 2010s that changed. This page uses 622 campaigns from 1900 to 2019 to test three explanations for the drop.

Main findings

  1. Nonviolent campaigns succeeded 64% of the time in the 1990s and 35% in the 2010s, even as their number more than doubled.
  2. Most of the recent drop is a scoring rule. Campaigns still under way in 2019 are counted as failures; among those that had finished, 50% succeeded.
  3. Crowding explains little. Campaigns that overlap with others in the same country do win less, but mostly because overlap happens in countries where winning is hard anyway.
  4. Falling freedom does not explain it. Campaigns do better where freedom has been rising, but no more of them now start where it is falling than did in the 2000s.
  5. Where campaigns happen matters somewhat. More of them now take place in regions where success has always been lower.

About the data

Campaigns. The NAVCO 1.3 list compiled by Erica Chenoweth and Christopher Wiley Shay (Harvard Dataverse). It covers 622 mass campaigns from 1900 to 2019 with a "maximalist" goal: removing a government, ending a foreign occupation, or secession. Each is coded as primarily nonviolent or violent, and as a success, a limited success, a failure, or ongoing. Here "succeeded" means full success only.

Freedom index. The Civil Liberties Index from the V-Dem Institute, which scores every country in every year from 0 (least free) to 1 (most free). Each campaign is matched to its country's score in the year it began; 617 of the 622 have one. Where V-Dem has no score for a territory in that year, the governing state's score is used.

Choices made here. The source file has no regions, so each country was assigned to one of five by hand. Three campaigns that began in 1899 are counted in 1900. "Starting" counts a campaign once, in the period it began, with its final outcome whenever that came. "Active" counts it in every period from start to end. Freedom "falling" or "rising" means a change of more than 0.05 over the five years before the campaign began. A success rate is shown only for groups of at least 5 campaigns unless a chart says otherwise. Campaigns still under way in 2019 are counted as failures unless a chart's switch is set to leave them out, and the written takeaways describe each chart as it first appears.

Limits. Groups get small quickly once the data is split three ways, so single-decade figures for small groups can swing widely. The dataset sometimes records one conflict as several campaigns, which inflates overlap. The comparisons show what goes together, not what causes what.
